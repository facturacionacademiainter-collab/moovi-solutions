The problem

At most companies, AI comes in through twenty different doors. One department buys an assistant, another builds its own bot, a third wires model calls into a spreadsheet. Six months later there are ten tools that don't talk to each other, none of them knows who is asking, and nobody can say what data each one saw.

The underlying problem isn't the model: it's governance. Who can ask what, which data answers it, which actions an agent can take without a person approving them, and how to reconstruct afterwards what happened. Kognia exists so that this gets solved once, in one place, instead of twenty times badly.

How it works

Capability registry

Each department publishes what its system can do: check balances, issue a delivery note, open a ticket, search the legal repository. Each capability declares which data it touches, which permissions it requires and whether its effect can be undone.

Request router

When someone asks something, the orchestrator breaks down the request, chooses which agents take part and in what order, and assigns them a compute budget. If the plan includes an irreversible action, it pauses and asks for human approval.

Permission-aware memory

The knowledge index stores, alongside each fragment, who is allowed to read it. Search filters by the identity of the person asking before retrieval, not after: data you aren't entitled to never enters the context.

Architecture decision

Orchestration is separate from the agents, and the agents are separate from the tools. An agent doesn't know how to connect to the ERP: it requests a capability by name and the registry handles the connection, the permission and the retry. That makes it possible to switch model providers, or replace an entire system's integration, without touching any agent's logic. Every execution is stored as a call tree with its inputs, outputs and cost, so an answer from three months ago can be reconstructed exactly as it was produced.

The problem

Companies accumulate models that predict: likelihood to buy, to churn, to default. But a number isn't a decision. Between “this customer has a 62% chance of churning” and “call them Tuesday and offer plan B” there's a leap that almost always ends up being settled by a monthly meeting and a spreadsheet of rules nobody dares to touch.

That's where the value gets lost. Rules go stale, nobody measures whether the action worked, and when someone proposes changing the criteria there's no way to know what will happen short of testing it on real customers. Arbitrium turns that leap into a piece of software: an explicit policy that is versioned, measured and reversible.

How it works

Action catalog

Every possible action is declared with its cost, its available capacity (how many calls fit in a day) and its constraints: who it can't be offered to, how many times per month, what requires approval.

Learned policy

From historical data, the engine learns which action works best for each type of case, and keeps learning in production by reserving a fraction of traffic to explore alternatives. It doesn't optimize the prediction: it optimizes the outcome.

Explanation and control

Every recommendation comes with the signals that weighed most and the expected outcome. Hard business rules live outside the model, in a file the business team can read and change without asking IT for anything.

The problem

Big decisions (opening a warehouse, changing the inventory policy, taking out a loan to fund the season) are made with a spreadsheet that projects a single line. The spreadsheet knows nothing about queues, variability, or what happens when two things go wrong in the same week.

The result is a plan that works on paper and fails in reality for reasons nobody modeled: the supplier who runs late, the spike that saturates capacity, the correlation between two variables that were looked at separately. Réplica puts that variability inside the model, which is where it belongs.

How it works

Calibrated twin

The operation is modeled as a system of resources, queues and events: orders arriving, capacity being used, times that vary. The parameters aren't made up: they're estimated from the company's real historical data.

Scenarios

Thousands of runs are executed on that twin, changing whatever you want to test. The output isn't a number but a distribution: the typical case, the bad one and the very bad one, each with its probability.

What moved the needle

A sensitivity analysis ranks variables by how much of the difference between scenarios they explain, so you know where it pays to invest in reducing uncertainty and where it isn't worth it.

The problem

A serious investigation starts with a box of material: case files, emails, meeting minutes, bank statements, spreadsheets. Reading all of it takes weeks and, even after reading everything, the connection that matters usually sits between two documents nobody looked at together.

Search tools don't help, because they search for words, not people. “Juan P. Gómez”, “Gómez, J.”, “JP Gómez” and a stray tax ID are the same person, and no text search knows it. Indicio first resolves who is who, and only then lets you ask questions.

How it works

Ingestion and normalization

Any format goes in: scanned PDFs, emails, spreadsheets, transcribed audio. Everything is stored with its text, its origin and its page, so it can be cited later.

Entities and links

People, companies, accounts, addresses, dates and amounts are extracted, and spelling variants are merged into a single entity. The graph is built on top of that: who appears with whom, in which document and in what role.

Cited report

Questions are answered over the graph and the corpus, and every claim in the report links to the document and the page. If there's no verifiable source, the claim isn't made.

The problem

A typical back-office process touches four systems: it's entered in the ERP, an email goes out, the CRM gets updated and someone approves. When it's automated with loose scripts, it works until the day the third step fails. Then two systems are updated and two aren't, and someone has to fix it by hand without really knowing what got through.

That's the hard problem in automation: not connecting systems, but keeping them consistent when something breaks midway. None of those systems shares a transaction with the others, so consistency has to be built.

How it works

Declared workflows

The process is defined as a sequence of steps with their conditions, waits and human approvals. It reads like the procedure that already existed on paper, not like code.

Durable execution

State is saved after every step. If the process goes down, it resumes where it left off; if a step is retried, the operation key keeps it from running twice.

Compensation

Each step can declare how it's undone. When the process fails further along, the engine runs the compensations in reverse order until everything is back to how it was before it started.

The problem

Most companies' cash projection is a single line: what they expect to collect, month by month, assuming everything goes the way it always does. It doesn't say how far it could deviate or what happens if it does, which is exactly what you need to know to make a decision.

And delinquency is viewed as a traffic light: current, 30, 60, 90. That light says where an account is today, not where it's heading. Two customers with the same days past due can end up in completely different places, and treating them the same costs money either way.

How it works

Scoring with a horizon

Instead of “will they default?”, the model answers “when?”. Survival curves give the probability of defaulting at 30, 60 and 90 days, which is what lets you anticipate instead of react.

Cash with bands

The projection combines what's committed, what's estimated and the historical variability of each segment. It returns a range with its probability, not a promise of a single number.

Stress testing

You define the shock (delinquency rises three points, a sector collapses, the rate changes) and the engine propagates it from each customer to the consolidated result.

The problem

Traditional auditing looks at a sample, and looks at it after the fact. If the fraud is in the 2% nobody reviewed, it gets discovered next year or never.

What's more, the fraud that matters is almost never an isolated odd transaction: it's a pattern. Invoices systematically split to stay under the approval limit, a supplier sharing bank details with an employee, approvals that always happen after hours. How it works

Normal-behavior profile

Forensic rules

Alongside the model run hard rules that encode known schemes: purchase splitting, duplicate suppliers, matching bank accounts, self-authorization.

Segregation of duties

It analyzes the ERP's actual permission graph, not the stated org chart, to find who can close an entire loop without anyone else being involved.

The problem

Most stores' search fails exactly when it matters most: when people type the way they talk instead of the way the catalog was entered. “Shoes for running on dirt” finds nothing if the product says “trail running footwear.”

And cart recovery is usually a flat coupon for everyone. Half of them get margin handed over when they were going to buy anyway, and for the other half the coupon isn't enough to tip the decision. It's money lost in both directions at the same time.

How it works

Search that understands

The catalog is indexed by meaning, not by words. The query is interpreted, filtered by what's in stock and ranked by combining relevance, availability and margin.

Conversational sales assistant

The problem

When a student gets stuck on a topic, the problem is almost never in that topic. It's three steps back, in something that stayed shaky and nobody caught because the class kept moving forward anyway.

A teacher with thirty students can't track that case by case. And platforms that promise “personalized learning” usually just reshuffle the order of exercises, without any real model of what each student knows.

How it works

Student model

For each concept, it estimates the level of mastery and how it fades over time. That model updates with every answer, not with every test.

The right exercise

When someone gets it wrong, the prerequisite graph makes it possible to step back to the weak concept that explains it and work there. Then it returns to the original topic.

Grading and feedback

Open-ended work is graded against the rubric with written feedback, and the teacher sees the course map: which topics are weak and for how many students.

Architecture decision

The prerequisite graph is content, not code: it's built and maintained by the teaching team, not the technical team. That separation is what lets the same platform serve high school math and regulatory compliance training without rewriting anything.
